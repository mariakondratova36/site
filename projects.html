Dissertation

The Eastern Genre in Soviet Cinema of the 1970s

A study of how Soviet cinema reframed the conventions of the American Western through collective memory, nostalgia, and masculinity.

Awarded Distinction

“An Abundance of Mythologisation, Nostalgia, and Masculinity: The Eastern Genre in Soviet Cinema of the 1970s”

The dissertation explored the evolution of the Soviet Eastern, a film genre that adapts and reframes the conventions of the American Western within a Soviet ideological framework. Through a focus on collective memory, nostalgia, and masculinity, it argues that, in the era of stagnation, Soviet identity became deeply rooted in representing the past as an instrument for interpreting the present. A visual analysis of film narratives demonstrates how the Eastern reflected and reinforced cultural tensions of the 1970s and offers a new perspective on the role of film in shaping and articulating Soviet identity.

Migration Portfolio

Reframing Migration

Two essays placing contemporary visual materials in dialogue with histories and debates about migration.

Awarded Distinction

“Reframing Migration: The U.S.-Mexico Border from Multiple Perspectives” and “Understanding the Contemporary through the Past: Zacheta National Gallery’s Perspective on Polish Migration”

The two essays placed contemporary materials in dialogue with discourse about migration. The first examined five visual artefacts — artworks, everyday objects, photographs, activist graphics, films, and digital media — representing the U.S.-Mexico Border, arguing that borders are not only fixed or concrete technologies but can also be immaterial and conceptual. The second reflected on a recent exhibition at the Zacheta National Gallery that explicitly addressed migration.

Art History Research Paper

Tissot’s Western Lens

A study of collecting, Japonaiserie, and the commodification of Japanese culture in the work of James Tissot.

Tissot’s Western Lens: The Art of Collecting and Commodification of Japanese Culture

This paper examined the influence of Japanese culture on European society in the mid- to late-nineteenth century through the case study of French artist James Tissot. It explored Tissot’s fascination with Japan and his engagement with Japonaiserie through his collecting practices and the fetishisation of Japanese culture, considering how this manifested in the commodification of both Japanese objects and women. These ideas were visually analysed through two of Tissot’s Young Ladies Looking at Japanese Objects (1869) and La Japonaise au Bain (1864). The Young Ladies paintings were examined in relation to Tissot’s collecting practices and the concept of the cabinet of curiosities, exploring how the works construct relationships between gender, consumption, and the display of Japanese objects. La Japonaise au Bain was used to examine Tissot’s representation of Japanese culture through a European lens, particularly how the objectification and commodification of Japanese culture shifts when a Western woman is placed within the constructed space of the Other.

Film Research Paper

Race and Gender in Film Narratives about Music

A comparative study of race, representation, and music in Blues in the Night and Ma Rainey’s Black Bottom.

Race and Gender in Film Narratives about Music: Understanding Historical and Contemporary Blues and Jazz Film

This paper examined the relationship between race, representation, and music in the film noir genre, with particular attention to the ways in which Hollywood capitalised on the popularity of African American jazz and blues culture without necessarily addressing the representation or integration of Black characters on screen. Blues in the Night (1941) and Ma Rainey’s Black Bottom (2020) were analysed through scholarship on film noir and the convergence of race and sound in cinema, illuminating the intersection of race, gender, and music culture in film and how these relationships have developed over time. The two films share narratives driven by blues and jazz but diverge significantly in their treatment and understanding of race and representation. In Blues in the Night, African American musical culture is, to an extent, sanctified and positioned as a source of artistic inspiration for a white musician, while Ma Rainey’s Black Bottom explores the historical exploitation of African American musicians within the music industry of the 1920s. The latter centres on the conflict between Black characters Ma Rainey, portrayed by Viola Davis, and Levee Green, portrayed by Chadwick Boseman, using their competing ambitions and experiences to examine questions of race, ownership, exploitation, and artistic expression.
